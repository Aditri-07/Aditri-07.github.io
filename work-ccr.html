Counterparty Credit Risk Exposure Engine

Hull-White 1FMonte CarloEE / PFECVADV01Stress testingPython

An IMM-style Monte Carlo engine for a small book of corporate bonds and interest rate swaps. It simulates the short rate and credit spreads jointly, reprices every trade on every path at every future date, and rolls the result into exposure profiles, CVA, sensitivities and stress tests. Every number on this page comes from the repository's engine, run after the curve fix described below.

The engine

From a curve to CVA

  1. Bootstrap zero rates from par swap quotes; natural cubic spline for smooth forwards.DF(t) = e^(−z(t)·t)
  2. Hull-White short rate, with θ(t) fitted so the model reprices today's curve. Bonds priced in closed form on every path.dr = [θ(t) − a·r] dt + σ dW
  3. One systematic GBM spread factor; each issuer's spread scales off it, so spreads widen together.s_i(t) = s_i(0) · S(t)/S(0)
  4. Reprice the whole book on all 5,000 paths at all 41 dates; exposure is the positive part.E(t) = max(MtM(t), 0)
  5. EE is the mean, PFE the 95th and 99th percentile across paths, at each date.EE(t) = 𝔼[E(t)]
  6. Unilateral CVA, flat hazard from the credit triangle.CVA = LGD · Σ EE · DF · ΔPD, λ ≈ s / LGD
Exposure

Exposure profiles under stress

The swap netting set shows the classic hump: rate diffusion grows exposure, then the shrinking stream of remaining payments pulls it back to zero. The sawtooth is the semi-annual payment dates, each one knocking out a slice of value. Switch to the full book and the picture is dominated by bond principal, which steps down as each bond matures.

Stress testing

Where stress actually shows up

At the book level, peak PFE₉₉ barely moves: +6% and +8%. That is because bond principal, which is close to deterministic, swamps everything else. Isolate the swaps and the same scenarios raise peak exposure by 38% and 80%, and push the peak later. The 2020-style shock hits harder than 2008 despite smaller spread widening: rate vol more than doubles, and swaps carry no spread exposure in this setup.

CVA

Blending hides the risk

The quick approach treats the whole book as one counterparty at the average spread: $0.93M. Computing CVA name by name, each with its own exposure and default probability, gives $1.48M. CVA is a sum of products; blending turns it into a product of averages, which washes out exactly the case that matters, large exposure sitting with the widest-spread names.

Per-name CVA, base scenario. 97% comes from the bonds: holding a bond means the full principal is at risk if the issuer defaults, so its "exposure" is large and always positive. Strictly, that is issuer (jump-to-default) risk rather than counterparty exposure; swaps are the textbook CCR case.

Model validation

The bug the headline numbers hid

The first version produced plausible CVA and PFE. The paths underneath were broken: the mean simulated short rate jumped from 2.8% to 45% in a single quarter and stayed there. The zero curve was clamped flat below its first tenor (3M), which put a kink in the forward curve at exactly 0.25 years. Hull-White's θ(t) depends on the slope of the forward curve, so it spiked to 170% at that point, and the quarterly grid landed right on it.

Show
before fixafter fix

The fix was one line: let the spline run down to t = 0 instead of clamping. θ(t) now stays within ±2.4% and simulated rates track the curve at 3–5%. Because exposure on this book is dominated by bond principal, aggregate CVA only moved from $890K to $931K, which is exactly why it went unnoticed. Check the paths, not just the summary.

Scope

Known simplifications

  • Single curve for discounting and projection; no OIS/SOFR dual-curve split.
  • Flat hazard rate instead of a bootstrapped CDS survival curve.
  • One systematic spread factor; no idiosyncratic spread moves.
  • No wrong-way risk, no collateral or margining: uncollateralized exposure throughout.
  • Bonds and swaps netted together for illustration; real netting sets follow legal agreements, and held bonds are issuer risk.
  • Parallel DV01 only; no key-rate buckets.
  • Stress scenarios are parameter multipliers, not historical re-estimation.
