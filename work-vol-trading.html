Volatility Forecasting & IV Trading

GARCHSkewed Student-tBlack–Scholes–MertonRegression diagnosticsRPython

Options price a forward-looking volatility; returns carry a backward-looking one. This project backs implied volatility out of AAPL and WMT option prices, forecasts volatility with GARCH, and tests whether the gap between the two is stable enough to trade. Every number and chart below is recomputed in your browser from the project's data.

Group project for Financial Time Series at Rutgers, with three co-authors. Data: daily adjusted closes Apr 2022 – Apr 2025 and weekly ATM options expiring Dec 2024 – Apr 2025, from Bloomberg.

Underlying
Stylized facts

Returns are uncorrelated. Their squares are not.

Daily log returns show almost no autocorrelation, but squared returns do: big moves cluster. The Ljung–Box test on squared returns rejects white noise, which is the case for a conditional-variance model rather than a constant one.

Model selection

From ARCH(9) to GARCH(1,1)-skew-t

  1. PACF of squared returns cuts off at lag 9 for AAPL and lag 1 for WMT, so start with ARCH(9) and ARCH(1).
  2. Most ARCH(9) terms are insignificant; ARCH(1) residuals still show autocorrelation in squares, and Jarque–Bera rejects normality.
  3. Fat, skewed tails call for Student-t, then skewed Student-t innovations. Lingering ARCH effects in AAPL push to GARCH(1,1).
  4. GARCH(1,1)-skew-t passes residual diagnostics and has the lowest AIC for both names. Forecasts: rolling two-year windows, one step ahead.

AIC per observation, re-estimated for this page with Python's arch package on the same returns (the paper used R's fGarch; the ranking is the same). Lower is better.

Conditional variance

How long a shock lasts

In GARCH(1,1), tomorrow's variance is a weighted sum of a constant, today's squared shock and today's variance: σ²ₜ₊₁ = ω + α·aₜ² + β·σ²ₜ. Persistence α + β sets how slowly a shock fades. Hit the series with a shock and watch it decay.

Annualized volatility after a one-day shock of the chosen size, starting from the long-run level, against the fitted parameters. The dashed line is the unconditional volatility √(ω / (1 − α − β)).

Implied volatility

Backing σ out of a price

Black–Scholes–Merton has no closed-form inverse, so implied vol is a root-finding problem: find σ with BSM(σ) = market mid. Price is monotone in σ (vega > 0), so the root is unique. The project used Brent's method; here Newton steps on vega show the same root.

    Econometrics

    Does the forecast explain the implied vol?

    Regress BSM implied vol on the GARCH forecast for each name and option type, then put every fit through Gauss–Markov diagnostics. Most specifications fail. Two survive, and they survive in different shapes.

    Options
    Specification

    n = 17 weekly expiries per series. IV_GARCH is the one-day-ahead daily σ forecast; IV_BSM is annualized, so slopes absorb the √252 scaling. Durbin–Watson tests residual autocorrelation, Breusch–Pagan heteroskedasticity, Jarque–Bera normality (the paper also ran Shapiro–Wilk, and both selected models fail normality). The paper also checked stability with Chow and rolling Chow tests and CUSUM.

    Trading signal

    Fair-value IV, and what happens when you trade it

    The selected regression turns a GARCH forecast into a fair implied vol. When the market's IV sits above fair value by more than a threshold, sell volatility; below, buy it. The paper then backtested this on simulated IV paths with a 15% stop-loss.

    Series

    In-sample: fair value comes from a regression fitted on these same 17 points, so this shows the signal mechanics, not a forecast. The backtest results are from the paper: AAPL calls ended at $99,927.94 on $100,000 (−0.07%), WMT puts at $99,980.41 (−0.02%), before transaction costs. With 17 observations, the honest conclusion is that the relationship is fragile and the edge is not proven; the next steps are out-of-sample testing, delta-hedged vega exposure, and costs.
